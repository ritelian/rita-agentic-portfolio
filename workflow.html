THE DESIGN INTENT

Help people make a decision.
Then make that decision observable.

This is a self-initiated demonstration, created for this portfolio. The scenario, source text and review materials are fictional and do not reproduce an employer’s deliverables or internal resources.

01 / LEARNER NEED

A plausible summary
can still be wrong.

The fictional learner is a team lead starting to use AI to summarise workplace notes. The task is to decide whether a draft is safe to share.

The design hypothesis is that a short verification routine could help learners distinguish source evidence from confident-sounding additions. That hypothesis has not been tested with learners.

Proposed discovery

Ask learners to demonstrate how they currently check a summary. Explore their time constraints, access to source information and escalation options before prescribing training.

A boundary for learning

If the source is unavailable or approval responsibilities are unclear, a lesson alone will not resolve the problem. The workflow also needs access to evidence and a named reviewer.

Observable objective

Given a source note and a draft summary, identify unsupported claims, write a source-faithful revision and explain why each change is necessary.

02 / LEARNING JOURNEY

Model the reasoning.
Practise the decision.

  1. 01
    NOTICE

    Compare source and draft

    Present a short fictional note beside a deliberately inaccurate summary. Ask which statements the source actually supports.

  2. 02
    EXPLAIN

    Show the verification routine

    Break the draft into claims. Match each claim to evidence. Remove or flag anything unsupported. Keep the original uncertainty visible.

  3. 03
    APPLY

    Revise and justify

    Use a second fictional example for independent practice. Give feedback on both the revision and the reasoning, with an opportunity to try again.

For a facilitated session, discuss different revisions in pairs. For self-paced delivery, provide a worked example and feedback after the learner’s first attempt. These are proposed delivery options, not programmes already delivered.

03 / PRODUCTION DECISIONS

Define the handover.
Make review explicit.

This prototype uses a simple separation of responsibilities. Claude Code or Codex can support drafting and code development; the browser example itself runs local templates and calls no AI service.

PERSONAL DEMONSTRATIONDECISION → OUTPUT
Designer sets the task

Choose the intended behaviour, audience assumptions and evidence needed to judge performance.

Tools support production

Prepare a draft from the agreed inputs. Keep its status visible as unreviewed.

Reviewer checks the draft

Check learning alignment, source accuracy and usability. Return failures for revision.

Designer accepts the sample

Record the review decision. Any change to the brief invalidates that decision and requires another review.

For a larger implementation, GitHub could record proposed changes and review decisions. This demonstration does not claim to reproduce a production orchestration system or an organisation’s approval process.

04 / REVIEW EXAMPLE

Show why a draft
needs to change.

Fictional source note

The team discussed a pilot. A decision will follow a review next week. No budget or launch date was approved.

Deliberately flawed draft

The team approved the pilot and its budget. The launch will take place next week.

Three claims fail the evidence check

The source does not support approval of the pilot, approval of a budget or a launch next week. A revision needs to restore the pending decision and avoid adding certainty.

One acceptable revision

The team discussed a pilot and will review it next week before making a decision. No budget or launch date has been approved.

Useful feedback names the unsupported claim, points to the source and explains the correction. That gives the learner a repeatable method rather than just a score.

05 / EVALUATION PLAN

Measure performance.
Do not infer it from completion.

Immediate performance

Assess whether learners identify every unsupported claim, preserve the source meaning and justify the revision. Offer feedback and a second attempt where needed.

Transfer

Use a different fictional example later to see whether the verification method transfers. Record the types of errors, not only task completion.

Usability

Observe whether learners understand the instructions, can compare the two texts and can recover after a mistake. Check keyboard and assistive-technology use.

Operational fit

Ask reviewers whether the activity reflects the actual task and whether escalation guidance is clear. Adjust the learning approach when the context changes.

Evaluation is proposed. There are no learner-test results, productivity measurements or client outcomes associated with this demonstration.

06 / SCOPE & REFLECTION

A small prototype.
A visible design rationale.

The value of this example is the connection between a learner’s task, purposeful practice and inspectable feedback. The review interaction makes accountability visible without pretending that a checked box establishes quality.

The prototype is deliberately limited: it generates a fixed activity, does not call live agents and does not assess a learner’s response automatically. A changed objective may require a different activity; the human reviewer must decide whether the sample still fits.
