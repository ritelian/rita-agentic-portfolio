01 / VISITOR NEEDS

Help someone assess
fit and evidence.

The primary visitor is a recruiter or prospective collaborator exploring learning design, AI enablement and digital experience skills. The design assumes they need to understand the positioning quickly and inspect selected examples in more depth.

That is a design assumption, not a finding from completed user research. The proposed next step is to test whether visitors can identify the focus, distinguish fictional demonstrations from professional history and find supporting artefacts.

02 / INTERFACE DECISIONS

An editorial identity.
A practical hierarchy.

Preserve recognition

The original RM monogram and portrait anchor the identity. Ink, warm paper and restrained neutral panels give content a consistent visual setting.

Separate levels of reading

Short introductions establish intent. Section headings support scanning. Detail pages expose rationale, samples and limitations without overloading the homepage.

Make evidence accessible

Capability links take visitors directly to the relevant blueprint, review example or design explanation. Demonstration status appears beside the work.

Adapt the structure

Columns become a single reading order on smaller screens. Navigation uses an explicit menu control; long technical material can scroll within its own panel.

03 / REVIEW INTERACTION

Make state changes
understandable.

The fictional workflow prototype has an explicit sequence: define a brief, inspect a sample, acknowledge review criteria and accept it. Editing the brief invalidates the prior decision so the interface does not imply that an altered draft has already been reviewed.

Labels explain what each input controls. Status messages announce progress. The interface uses native form controls, keyboard focus indicators and text-based feedback. No information relies on colour alone.

The checklist models an interaction pattern; it cannot establish whether a person actually performed a meaningful review.

04 / NEXT EVALUATION

Test the decisions.
Refine the experience.

  • Ask a visitor to explain the professional focus after a short homepage visit.
  • Ask them to find the evidence for learning design and for UX/UI.
  • Check whether they recognise which materials are fictional and which describe experience.
  • Observe keyboard-only and screen-reader use of the review interaction.
  • Use the findings to revise the hierarchy, labels and navigation.

Responsive and interaction checks support implementation quality. They are not a substitute for usability research or a complete accessibility audit.
